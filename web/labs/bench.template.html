Comparing MATs and CATs

A measurement made in one observing condition (hereafter "condition": an observer under a light) often has to be carried to another. There are two different things to predict there, and two kinds of transform made for them.

  • Corresponding color: the color that looks the same, in the new condition, as the original did in its own, to a viewer adapted to each. It is about appearance, and is measured by asking observers. A chromatic adaptation transform (CAT) is made to predict it.
  • Material color: the color the same material will measure in the new condition. It is about the material, and is measured with an instrument. A material adjustment transform (MAT) is made to predict it.

The two are not the same: a material whose color changes with the light does not look the same to an adapted viewer under both, and its corresponding color is not what it measures. This page tests material color: it takes objects whose spectra are known, so that their colors under every light can be computed, and asks how close each transform comes to them. That is the MAT's job; for the CATs, it is a job they were not made for. How well they predict corresponding colors, their own job, needs data from observers and is not tested here (on such data, the Wpt paper found CAT02 ahead of the MAT). Here the observer stays the same and the light changes, as a CAT assumes; for a change of observer, where no CAT applies, the observer lab compares the MAT with other ways of converting an observer (Observers, section 4).

1. One shape for every transform

Every transform here works the same way. It takes a color measured under the first light, cmeasured, into a common representation, one meant to hold whatever the light, and brings it back out as the color it predicts under the second light, cpredicted:

cpredicted = Tdest−1 Tsrc cmeasured

Tsrc, for the first light (the source), takes the color into the common representation, and Tdest−1, for the second light (the destination), brings it out under that light. The transforms differ only in T, the matrix that takes a condition's colors into the common representation, and so in what the common representation holds:

TransformWhat its T does Built from
von Kries scalingscales each channel of XYZ so that the light's white becomes 1 the light's white point
Bradford, CAT02first turns XYZ into cone-like channels (each its own matrix), then scales them so that white becomes 1: a model of how the eye adapts to a light's whitethe light's white point
Wpt MATthe condition's Wpt normalization: it puts white at W = 100, then places a set of base materials (40 Munsell colors) where they belong in the reference's material coordinates, so that materials, not only white, land togetherthe light's spectrum, as the observer's sensors see it through those materials

That makes two families. White point based transforms (von Kries, Bradford, CAT02) are built from the two lights' white points alone: the color a perfect white measures under each. Spectrally based transforms (the Wpt MAT) are built from the two lights' whole spectra, through the colors materials measure under each. The difference shows most plainly with two lights of the same white point but different spectra, such as daylight and an LED made to match its white. To every white point based transform they are the same light: it predicts no change at all, for any material. Materials do change between such lights, by how far depends on their spectra, and a spectrally based transform sees that.

Every one of them carries white exactly: a perfect white under the first light comes out as a perfect white under the second. They differ in what they do to everything else. A CAT moves every color by the same rule that moves white, as an eye adapting to the new light would; that is what corresponding color asks for. A MAT moves each color as materials of that color move between the two lights; that is what material color asks for. A CAT keeps one observer and changes the light; the MAT can change the light, the observer or both, because its T is built for each condition from its own sensors. The sections below put the transforms to the test of material color: first for one material, then for many, then for many lights.

A challenge

Find two lights in the menus above whose white points nearly match but whose spectra differ. To every white point based transform they are nearly the same light, so its prediction hardly differs from the measurement it started from; but the material's measurement still moves between them. The line under the plots says how far apart the two white points are (Δu′v′, the distance in the CIE 1976 u′, v′ diagram) and how much the measurement moves and CAT02 changes it. Watch the swatches: the CATs' predictions stay with the first measurement, the MAT's goes with the second.

Some answers

A and LED-BH1, both near 2850 K (white points 0.0004 apart); LED-B1 and LED-V1, near 2730 K; D65 and LED-B5, daylight and a blue-pumped LED of about the same white; F2 and LED-V2, a fluorescent and a violet-pumped LED. Over the Munsell colors, from F2 to LED-V2 the measurements move by a mean ΔE00 of 4.3, CAT02's predictions are 3.9 from where they land and the MAT's 0.7.

The guide's numbers, checked

The guide to Wpt and WLab, WLab and Wpt: Color Without the Fine Print, quotes a result of the Wpt paper: over 5,162 measured reflectances, the Wpt MAT predicted D65 colorimetry (material color) from illuminant A more closely than CAT02 (mean ΔE00 1.24 against 2.35, CIE 1964 observer). Not all of the 5,162 are open data, so that test is not rerun here. The code that goes with the guide (wpt-onramp, its example ex05) reruns a smaller version on the 1,600 corrected Munsell colors (1.09 against 2.26, CIE 1931); this page gives the same, below, with the Munsell colors in place of this section's objects and illuminant A to D65. The Munsell colors include the 40 the Wpt normalization is built from.

5. Watching the transforms work

The formula of section 1, played out. A set of Munsell colors is measured in three conditions at once, each condition's points in its own color: the 40 base colors of the Wpt normalization, a hue circle at Value 5, and ten hue spokes, from gray outward, in three planes, at Values 2, 5 and 8. Press play: each condition's T takes its measurements, step by step, into the common representation, and then they come back out again. In the common representation, colors that are the same material should land together. With a white point based transform only white and the grays do; the other colors keep the spread their conditions gave them. With the Wpt MAT the materials land together, white and color alike, as far as the conditions allow.

Drag the view to turn it; the slider moves through the steps by hand. The scale follows the points, so they fill the view at every step (the extent of each axis is under the view). The view's vertical axis is the first channel of each step (W in Wpt), the other two across. Each condition's measurements are its sensor excitations, scaled so that white's second one is 100; a CAT's common representation is shown with white at 100, 100, 100.
