Lighting

A light changes the colors of the objects it falls on, and no two lights change them alike. An observing condition (hereafter "condition") is an observer under a light, and each condition has its own Wpt normalization, which puts its light's white at the same place: what is left is how the light renders the objects. This lab takes a test light and a reference light, and measures how far each object moves in WLab between the two, for many objects and many observers at once.

1. The test light

2. The reference light

The light the test light is compared with. By default, the Planckian (blackbody) or daylight light that renders the objects most like the test light does, found in section 4; any other light can be chosen.

3. How far objects move between the two lights

Take an object and an observer. The object's Wpt under the test light and under the reference light, each through the observer's own Wpt normalization for that light, would be the same if the two lights rendered it alike; the WLab difference between them (ΔEWLab) is how far the object moves between the two lights. The mean of that difference over every object and every observer is the Illuminant Rendering Equivalency (IRE). It is 0 when the two lights render every object alike for every observer, and the larger it is, the more the two lights differ in how they render objects. Because each normalization puts its light's white at the same Wpt, the IRE leaves out the color of the light itself.

Selecting objects and observers for the IRE

The objects are the materials the lights are compared on, and the observers those who see them; the more varied the objects' spectra, the more a difference between lights shows. They apply to sections 3 to 5.

Objects

A CGATS measurement file (with spectral fields such as SPECTRAL_NM380, as spectrophotometers and chart software write them) or a table: wavelength in the first column and one object per column, or one object per row with the wavelengths across the header row; values from 0 to 1 or in percent, covering at least 420 to 680 nm. A chart you measured yourself can be used this way. The file is read in this page and goes nowhere.

Observers

The objects that move most

The 15 objects with the largest mean ΔEWLab over the observers. The shares split each one's differences (their squares, summed over the observers) into lightness, chroma (toward or away from neutral) and hue (around neutral), along the object's own hue under the reference light; the three add to 100%.

Every object under the reference light in the aw–bw plane of WLab, and for the 15 that move most, an arrow to where the test light puts them, for the observer chosen. Hover over a ring for the object.

Observer by observer

Each row is one observer's share of the IRE: the WLab differences of the objects between the two lights for that observer.

4. The closest Planckian and daylight references

The IRE of the test light against every Planckian light from 1750 to 12000 K and every CIE daylight from 4000 to 14000 K, in steps of 250 K. The lowest point of each line is the reference of that kind that renders the objects most like the test light does. It need not be at the test light's correlated color temperature: the IRE compares renderings, not the color of the lights.

5. Many test lights at once

The same for a set of test lights: for each, the closest Planckian and the closest daylight reference and their IRE, the smaller of the two in italics, and the curves, colored by the kind of light.
