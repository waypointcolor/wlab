Metamers and material color equivalency

Two materials can match exactly under one light, for one viewer, and still be different materials: they reflect light differently, wavelength by wavelength. Such a pair are metamers. Change the light or the viewer and the match can come apart.

Any material has many such matches. Under a given light, for a given viewer, they all share its material color (its Wpt): they are equivalent in material color there, which is what this page's title means. On this page you can:

  • see one such pair come apart, and compare how far each material moves in CIELAB and in WLab (section 1);
  • make matches for a material and see how far they come apart under other lights and for other viewers (section 3);
  • ask the matches to hold under several lights or for several viewers at once, and see how much closer they then stay everywhere else (section 4);
  • go the other way, from color values (such as L*, a* and b*) to one realistic reflectance that gives them (section 5);
  • take any of them to the Objects lab, which compares pairs light by light (section 6).

An observing condition (hereafter "condition") is an observer, a viewer or a camera's sensors, under a light. What turns a material into one of its metamers is a metameric black: a change in reflectance, up at some wavelengths and down at others, that the condition's sensors do not respond to at all. Added to the material, it leaves the measurement, and so the Wpt, unchanged in that condition, but generally not in others.

3. Metamers for one condition

The metameric blacks used here are those real materials carry. Take the 1,600 corrected Munsell colors, remove from each the part the condition's sensors respond to, and what is left of each is a metameric black for that condition. The shapes those leftovers take most often (the leading eigenvectors of their spread) are the blacks a metamer is made from here, each in proportion to how much the Munsell colors carry it. Each metamer is the material plus a random mix of them, taken a random part of the way toward where its reflectance would leave 0 to 1, so that every metamer is a reflectance a material could have.

The table: for each condition looked in, the ΔEWLab of each metamer from the material, their mean and the largest. In the condition matched every difference is 0 (to the last digit shown). The plot: the material (ringed) and its metamers in WLab, in the condition matched (left) and the one chosen (right), each dot in the metamer's own display color in that condition.

4. Metamers for several conditions at once

Asking a metamer to match in more conditions at once leaves it fewer metameric blacks: each must give no response to the sensors of every condition matched. Fewer reflectances qualify, and those that do come apart less in the conditions not matched. The same recipe, from the corrected Munsell colors, gives the blacks for any set of conditions.

Each column matches the condition of section 2 and the extra conditions up to it, in order; each cell is the mean ΔEWLab of that set of metamers from the material, with the largest in brackets. A condition matched reads 0. Each column's metamers are made from the same random draws, so the columns differ only in the blacks allowed.

5. From color values to a reflectance

The other way round: start from color values, the three numbers that place a color in a color space (such as L*, a* and b* in CIELAB), and find one realistic reflectance that gives those values. Give the values as they are in the condition of section 2, in any of the four spaces below. Whichever space you use, the page first converts them to Wpt values in that condition, because the reflectance is built in Wpt.

How the reflectance is built. Any reflectance is a gray part, the same at every wavelength, plus a selective part that gives it its hue. Rescaled to run from 0 to 1, the selective part is the reflectance's characteristic reflectance. Adding gray to a characteristic reflectance, or scaling it, changes its Wpt lightness and chroma but not its hue, in any condition. So the page takes a characteristic reflectance with the hue asked for and works out the two amounts, gray g and selective s, that give the lightness and chroma asked for. The reflectance is g + s × the characteristic reflectance.

Where the characteristic reflectances come from. Each of the 40 Munsell hues gives one: that of its most vivid corrected Munsell color, rescaled over 420 to 680 nm (where the eye is sensitive) and kept within 0 to 1. A hue that falls between two of them gets a mix of the two, in the proportion that gives exactly that hue.

The conversion to Wpt is exact: WLab and WLch by undoing WLab's steps; CIELAB and CIELCh by going back to the condition's XYZ, against its white, and then through its Wpt normalization. Changing the space rewrites the same values in the new one. The mix of two characteristic reflectances hits the hue exactly because Wpt is linear: the mix's Wpt lies on the straight line between theirs. Spread: the mean ΔEWLab, over the conditions looked in, between each reflectance there and in the condition of section 2.
