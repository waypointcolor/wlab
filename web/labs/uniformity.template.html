Uniformity and hue

WLab is meant to be a uniform space for material color: equal distances in it should be seen as about equal color differences, and a line of one WLab hue should keep one hue as its colors get lighter and grayer. This page looks at that claim beside CIELAB and CAM16-UCS, with numbers and pictures, and leaves the judging to you.

An observing condition (hereafter "condition") is an observer under a light. WLab describes the color of a material, whatever the condition; CIELAB and CAM16-UCS describe a color in one condition. Where they are compared below, theirs are the chosen condition's coordinates of the material the WLab color stands for. CAM16-UCS is the uniform color space of the CIE's color appearance model CAM16, computed here for an adapting luminance of 100 cd/m², a background of Yb 20, an average surround and full adaptation to the condition's white. The page opens on CIE 1931 (2°) under illuminant C, the condition of the Munsell renotation and of WLab's own reference.

1. Constant hue

Each row is one lightness, from gray (left) to the most vivid color your screen can show. Each chip is split: the lower-right half holds WLab hue constant; the upper-left half has the same lightness and chroma in the space it is compared with (CIELAB or CAM16-UCS, in the chosen condition) but holds that space's hue constant, at the hue of the ringed chip. Judge for yourself which half stays closer to the ringed chip's hue as the colors get lighter and grayer.

Both halves show the material each color stands for as it would look under D65, the screen's white, so that only the hue line differs between them. The observers are the CIE color-matching functions, for which CIELAB and CAM16 are defined.

2. Munsell loci

The Munsell system was built by eye so that its steps look even: its Hues are lines of constant hue, and its Chromas rings of equal color strength. Here are the corrected Munsell colors of one Value (lightness) in WLab, in CIELAB and in CAM16-UCS: each Hue's chips joined from gray outward (a spoke), and each Chroma's chips joined around the circle (a ring). A space that holds hue constant along Munsell's Hues draws the spokes straight, and one whose steps are even draws the rings round and evenly spaced.

Each dot is a chip in the color it has under D65; hover over one for its notation. The dashed pattern is the same chips in WLab's reference condition, CIE 1931 under C. The tour shows each condition for the time chosen (a longer time leaves room to read the summary), with each plot's axes fixed for the whole tour, and goes round until it is stopped or a condition is chosen by hand.

Why WLab's rings crowd together toward the outside: WLab's chroma is a logarithm of Wpt's chroma, so each further step of chroma in Wpt adds less in WLab, by an amount that depends on hue and lightness. WLab is shaped so that a straight-line distance in it reads like ΔE00, which counts a chroma difference between vivid colors as smaller than the same difference between dull ones; CIELAB leaves that to ΔE00's weighting, and WLab builds it into the space. Munsell's Chroma steps were judged as large steps along a scale, while ΔE00 rests on judgments of small differences between near matches, and the two do not agree on how far apart vivid colors are; the steps column of the Hue by Hue table puts a number on it. The same compression evens out the rings' chroma from hue to hue. A large-scale form of WLab, LSWLab, undoes it, for large steps: Large and small color differences.

What the tour shows

Tour the lights and watch the three plots. In WLab the pattern of chips stays almost where it is, over the dashed reference, in every condition: the spokes keep their directions, the rings keep their shapes, and neighboring chips keep their spacing. WLab describes the material, and a material keeps its place whatever the light and whoever is looking; a chip moves only as far as its color really changes between the conditions. That is material color equivalency, and it is about more than hue: chroma, the distances between materials and the shape of the whole pattern carry over from one condition to the next.

In CIELAB and CAM16-UCS the whole pattern moves and changes shape from light to light: it shifts off center, spokes swing round and bend, rings stretch one way and shrink another, and the gaps between neighbors grow in one part of the circle and close in another. Those spaces describe each chip's color in one condition, as that condition's observer sees it under that light (CAM16-UCS as a viewer adapted to the light would see it), and that is what they are made to do; it follows that a distance between two materials measured in them holds for the one condition it was measured in. The mean move of a chip from the reference, in the summary above, puts a number on each.

Hue by Hue

For each Hue at this Value, the hue angles of its chips: their spread from the smallest to the largest, and their RMS about their mean. 0 means every chip of the Hue has one hue angle. Steps: how evenly chroma grows along the Hue, the chroma gained per step of Munsell Chroma from chip to chip, as the RMS of those gains about their mean, in percent of the mean; 0% means every step adds the same chroma.

Chroma by Chroma

For each Chroma at this Value with chips at four Hues or more, the chroma of its chips round the circle in each space: their RMS about their mean, in percent of the mean, so that the three spaces' scales read alike. 0% means the ring is at one chroma all the way round, as Munsell's Chromas, judged by eye under one viewing condition, were made to be: chips of one Chroma look equally strong whatever their Hue.

3. The object-color solid

No material can be more chromatic than the colors at the surface of the object-color solid: those of reflectances that are 0 or 1, jumping between them once or twice across the spectrum. Each condition has its own solid. In WLab, which describes materials whatever the condition, the solids of two conditions would be the same if the two conditions told the same set of materials apart; where they differ, one condition gives more room to some colors than the other does.
