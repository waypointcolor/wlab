Robustness report: will a match hold?

A match that passes in the viewing booth can part under store or home lighting. Paste the spectral reflectance of a standard and its samples, choose the lights (and observers) where the product will be seen, and read how far each sample is from the standard under each one, as ΔEWLab: one table to compare submissions, suppliers or formulations, or to hand to a supplier. Everything runs in this page; nothing is sent anywhere.

For buyers and managers, with no programming needed. How to ask for it and how to read it are in the guide's section "For buyers and managers" (Wpt and WLab: Color Without the Fine Print). For more than one standard, many lights and observers at once, and how each material moves, the Materials across lights and observers lab goes further.

1. Spectra

One line per material: a first line of wavelengths in nm, then a line for each material, its name first; the first material is the standard. To add a sample, add a line; to drop one, delete its line. Comma, tab or semicolon separated. Any range and interval from 380 to 780 nm; reflectance as 0–1 or in percent. A table with one material per column (wavelengths down the first column) is read too; the line under the box says which way it was read.

    4. How to read this report

    • The first row is ΔE00 between each sample and the standard under the reference condition, normally the viewing booth: the familiar check, as an instrument in the booth reports it.
    • Every other row is ΔEWLab between each sample and the standard under that light (and observer, where one is named; otherwise the CIE 1931 standard observer). Read each number like a ΔE: they are designed to be similar in size, and 0 means the sample and the standard give the same color values there.
    • Look for patterns more than single numbers. A sample that stays small under every light holds its match. A sample that is small in the booth and jumps under one kind of light is a metamerism risk under that light: the two materials match only where they were matched. Compare the samples row by row, under the lights the product will really meet.
    • There are no built-in tolerances, just as ΔE00 and ΔE*ab tolerances differ from one industry to the next. Let experience with your own products set the limits, and keep your booth ΔE00 specification as it is.
    • What it does not say. ΔEWLab is a difference between materials: the color difference the reference observer (CIE 1931, under illuminant C) would see between them, whatever light and observer they were measured under. It is not a prediction of how the two will look to someone standing in the store; that is the job of a color appearance model.
    • Standard lights stand in for real ones. A "D50" booth only approximates D50, and store lights vary. For the lights that matter most, add their measured spectra on the report's page, or choose the measured booth simulators in its list of lights. If viewers of different ages matter, add a second observer, such as the CIE 1964 (10°) one.
    • Next steps. Use it to choose between submissions, suppliers or formulations that all pass in the booth, to know in advance which lights are likely to bring complaints, and to ask for spectra of production samples, not only of the approved submission.

    ΔEWLab predicts the color difference the reference observer (CIE 1931, under illuminant C) would see between two materials, whatever light and observer they were measured under, in units that behave like familiar ΔE units. No tolerances are built in: as with ΔE00, experience with your own products should set the limits. Chips are approximate sRGB renderings under D65.
